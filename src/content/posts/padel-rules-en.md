You've booked your first padel session. Maybe you've watched a few matches, maybe you haven't — and suddenly someone shouts "30-30," the ball comes flying off the glass, and a point gets replayed for reasons you can't quite follow. It can sound like a completely different language.

The good news: padel's rules are far easier than they look. Once you understand scoring, the serve, and a handful of wall rules, you'll be able to follow any match — and step onto the court for your first games with confidence instead of guesswork.

## How Scoring Works in Padel

### Points: 0, 15, 30, 40

Padel uses the same point system as tennis. The first point won is 15, the second is 30, the third is 40, and the fourth wins the game — but you must win by two clear points.

| Points won | Score |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 15 |
| 2 | 30 |
| 3 | 40 |
| 4 | Game |

At 40-40 (deuce), the game continues until one side wins two points in a row: advantage, then game. If the opponents take the next point, the score returns to deuce.

### Games, Sets, and the Match

Six games win a set, and most padel matches are the best of three sets. Win two sets and the match is yours. A typical recreational match takes between 60 and 90 minutes, which makes the format easy to fit into an evening.

### The Tie-Break

At 6-6, the set is decided by a tie-break — the same format used in tennis. The first player or team to reach 7 points wins the set, provided the lead is at least 2 points. If the score reaches 6-6 in the tie-break, play continues until one side leads by two. The tie-break exists so sets can't stretch on forever.

## The Serve: Padel's Most Unique Rule

The serve is where padel differs most dramatically from tennis. Everything about it is designed to slow the point down and keep rallies alive, rather than ending the point in one shot.

- **Underhand only:** the ball is bounced, then struck below waist level. Overhead serves are not allowed.
- **Bounce first:** the server bounces the ball on the court behind the service line before hitting it.
- **Diagonal box:** the serve must land in the diagonally opposite service box.
- **One bounce on the receiving side:** after crossing the net, the ball must bounce once inside the box before the receiver can play it.
- **No walls on the serve:** the ball cannot touch any wall before landing in the correct box.

### The Let Serve

If the ball clips the top of the net but still lands in the correct service box, it's a let — the serve is replayed with no penalty. However, stepping on or over the service line (a foot fault) loses the point outright.

## Faults: What Ends a Rally

In padel, the ball must always bounce on the court surface before touching any wall — and it can only hit the opponents' walls after bouncing in their half.

| Fault | Why it's a fault |
|---|---|
| Double bounce | The ball bounces twice before you return it |
| Wall before bounce | The ball hits a wall without touching the court first |
| Your own wall | The ball bounces in your half, then hits your wall before crossing |
| Out of court | The ball lands outside the lines without touching a wall first |
| Second bounce | You hit the ball after it has already bounced twice |

One detail that surprises new players: a ball that touches the net and carries over is perfectly legal in padel — the rally simply continues. Only if it clips the net and falls back on your side do you lose the point.

## The Walls: Your Extra Teammate

The walls are what make padel unique. After the ball bounces in your half, you may let it rebound off the back or side walls before returning it — and the ball can also rebound off the opponents' walls after bouncing in their half, which is why defending near the back glass is such a prized skill.

| Situation | Rule |
|---|---|
| Ball bounces in your half | You may let it touch your back or side wall, then return it |
| Ball hits your wall first, then crosses | Fault — point lost |
| Ball bounces in the opponents' half and hits their wall | Still in play — chase it |
| Ball flies over the walls | Out of play — point lost |

A few basics to round it out: padel is almost always played in doubles, with two players per side. Each player serves for a full game before the serve passes to the opposing team, and partners alternate receiving positions so the rotation stays fair.

Once the rules click, padel becomes dramatically more enjoyable — you stop second-guessing and start anticipating. And the better you understand the game, the clearer it becomes that your equipment should match your style: a control-oriented game thrives with a round racket, while aggressive wall play rewards a diamond-shaped power frame. Knowing the rules is the first step; choosing the right tool for how you actually play is the second.
