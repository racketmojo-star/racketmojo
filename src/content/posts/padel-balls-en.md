Every rally in padel starts and ends with the same object: the ball. Yet most players spend an hour choosing a racket and three seconds grabbing whatever tube sits on the club shop counter. That's backwards. The ball is the one piece of equipment you touch on every single point, and a dead or mismatched ball quietly sabotages your game before you even swing.

The good news: choosing the right ball is simple once you understand two things — what makes a padel ball different from a tennis ball, and how fast it loses its magic.

## Padel Balls vs Tennis Balls: Small But Significant

Padel balls look like tennis balls, but they are a different animal. Official padel balls are **slightly smaller and have lower internal pressure** than tennis balls — the exact combination that gives padel its slower, more controllable rallies and its characteristic soft feel off the wall.

| Property | Padel ball | Tennis ball |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| Diameter | 6.35–6.77 cm | 6.54–6.86 cm |
| Weight | 56.0–59.4 g | 56.0–59.4 g |
| Internal pressure | Lower | Higher |
| Bounce (254 cm drop) | 135–145 cm | 135–147 cm |

That lower pressure matters more than any other spec. It lets the ball sink slightly into the racket strings and the glass, which is why padel rewards touch and placement over raw power. Try playing tennis with a padel ball and you'll feel the difference in the very first exchange.

## The Short Life of a Pressurized Ball

Official padel balls are pressurized: the air sealed inside is what creates the bounce. From the moment you break the seal on the tube, that pressure starts escaping through the felt and the valve. There's no way to stop it, only to manage it.

| | Fresh ball | After 4–6 hours of play |
|---|---|---|
| Bounce | Lively and consistent | Flat, "dead" feel |
| Off the wall | Predictable | Drops early, hard to time |
| Arm feel | Comfortable | Heavy and jarring |

Plan to replace your balls **every 4–6 hours of play** — in practice, every one or two sessions. A quick drop test tells you the truth: hold the ball at shoulder height, let it go, and watch the bounce. If it comes back noticeably lower than when the tube was new, it's done. Playing with dead balls feels like hitting with a tired rubber band, and worse, it teaches you to adjust your technique to a ball that's gone.

## How to Choose: What Actually Matters

### Pressurized vs. Pressureless

Pressureless balls are usually sold for training and keep their bounce much longer because they have no internal pressure to lose. They're acceptable for casual hitting, but they feel heavier and faster off the wall — most players notice the difference within a single rally.

### Felt Quality and Color

Better felt means more grip for spin and a longer useful life. Cheap felt goes shiny and slick after one session; premium felt holds its texture for several. As for color, yellow is the easiest to track on most courts and remains the tournament standard, with white and orange approved for specific conditions.
